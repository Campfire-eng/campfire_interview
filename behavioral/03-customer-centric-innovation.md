# Customer-Centric Innovation

> Continuously improve our product based on customer needs and what we see as the most
> valuable next feature for them.

## What we are listening for

Our customers are controllers and accounting teams. They are not consumers, they are not
developers, and they are not us. An engineer who has never watched one do a month-end
close is likely to build the wrong thing, however well it is built. We want people who go
and look, who can tell the difference between what a customer asks for and what they
need, and who can argue for a feature on the customer's behalf, including when it is not
what they would prefer to build.

The principle has two halves and we ask about both. "Based on customer needs" is about
listening. "What we see as the most valuable next feature" is about judgment. Sometimes
the customer has not asked for the thing that will help them most, and we build it anyway.

The specific things we listen for:

- Direct contact with users: watching them, reading their tickets, talking to them, not
  just reading a spec written by someone who did.
- Translating a request into the problem underneath it.
- Saying no to a customer request in a considerate way, and being able to explain why.
- Building something nobody asked for because the evidence said it mattered.
- Empathy for a user under pressure, such as the controller at 11pm on the last day of
  close.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a feature you built where the customer asked for one thing and you built something different. How did you know, and how did it go?

**Why we ask it.** This is the judgment half. A request is often a symptom of a deeper
problem. We want to see them find that problem.

**What a strong answer covers.** A concrete request, the conversation or observation that
showed the underlying problem, and what they built instead. Most important: how they
checked with the customer before or after. Whether it solved the problem, and how they
know.

**Follow-ups.** "How did you find out what they needed?" "Did you tell them you were
building something different, or did they find out when it shipped?" "Did they agree it
was better?"

**Weaker answers.** They overrode the customer on instinct and never checked. The
"different thing" was what the engineer wanted to build. They cannot say whether it
worked.

### 2. Tell me about the last time you watched a user use your software. What did you see that surprised you?

**Why we ask it.** The strongest sign of customer focus is whether they have done this at
all, and how recently.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific session, such as a customer call, a support
shadowing, a usability session, or a screen recording. Something specific they saw that
they had not expected, and what they changed because of it. Small findings are good here:
a button nobody could find, a workflow done in a spreadsheet next to the product.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you change?" "How often do you do this?" "What did you see that
you have not fixed yet?"

**Weaker answers.** They have not done this, or the last time was years ago. They watched
but nothing surprised them. The observation did not lead to a change.

### 3. Tell me about a time you had to say no to a customer request. How did you say it, and how did they respond?

**Why we ask it.** Customer-centric does not mean customer-driven. A product that says yes
to everything becomes hard to use. We want to see them decline in a considerate way.

**What a strong answer covers.** A request that was reasonable from the customer's side
but wrong for the product, such as a one-off, a workaround, or something that would break
for other customers. They explained the reasoning in the customer's terms, offered what
they could do, and kept the relationship. They can say whether the customer was right in
the end.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you offer instead?" "Did they push back?" "Have you ever said no
and been wrong?"

**Weaker answers.** The no came from "not on the roadmap" with no reasoning. They have
never said no. They said no to one customer and yes to a louder one.

### 4. Describe a time you argued for building something the customers had not asked for. What was the evidence, and were you right?

**Why we ask it.** This is the second half of the principle. We want to see whether they
can make a customer-value case for something that has no ticket behind it.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific gap they observed, in usage data, in support
tickets, or in watching users, that customers had not put into words. How they made the
case. What they built. Whether customers used it, and how they measured that. An honest
answer where they were wrong is fine.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the evidence?" "Who did you have to convince?" "How did you
know afterward whether it mattered?"

**Weaker answers.** The evidence was "I thought it would be interesting to build". They
cannot say whether it was used. Every story ends with the customers loving it.

### 5. Tell me about a time a customer was frustrated with something you built. What did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Controllers under close pressure are direct. We want to see how they
handle an unhappy expert user without getting defensive and without giving in on
everything.

**What a strong answer covers.** They talked to the customer directly, or listened to the
call. They separated the frustration from the underlying issue. They fixed what could be
fixed, explained what could not, and followed up. They can describe what the customer
said in the customer's words.

**Follow-ups.** "What exactly did they say?" "Were they right?" "What did you tell them
you would do, and did you do it?"

**Weaker answers.** Support handled it. The customer was wrong and the story ends there.
They fixed it but never followed up with the customer.

### 6. How do you learn what a user's job actually looks like when it is nothing like yours?

**Why we ask it.** Most engineers we hire have never done a close. This question asks how
they close that gap, and whether they think it needs closing.

**What a strong answer covers.** Concrete habits, such as reading support tickets, sitting
in on onboarding, asking the customer-facing team, doing the workflow themselves in a
sandbox, or reading what practitioners read. An example of something they learned this
way that changed what they built. Awareness of how much they still do not know.

**Follow-ups.** "Give me an example of something you got wrong because you did not
understand the job." "What do you do in your first month on a new domain?"

**Weaker answers.** They rely entirely on the product manager. They assume the job is
simpler than it is. They have no example.
