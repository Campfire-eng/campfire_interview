# Customer-Centric Innovation

> Continuously improve our product based on customer needs and what we see as the most
> valuable next feature for them.

## What we are listening for

Our customers are controllers and accounting teams. They are not consumers, they are not
developers, and they are not us. An engineer who has never watched one do a month-end
close will build the wrong thing with great skill. We want people who go and look, who can
tell the difference between what a customer asks for and what they need, and who can
argue for a feature on the customer's behalf — including against their own preferences.

The principle has two halves and we test both. "Based on customer needs" is about
listening. "What we see as the most valuable next feature" is about judgment: sometimes
the customer has not asked for the thing that will help them most, and we build it anyway.

The specific things we listen for:

- Direct contact with users: watching them, reading their tickets, talking to them, not
  just reading a spec written by someone who did.
- Translating a request into the problem underneath it.
- Saying no to a customer request, well, and being able to explain why.
- Building something nobody asked for because the evidence said it mattered.
- Empathy for a user under pressure — the controller at 11pm on the last day of close.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a feature you built where the customer asked for one thing and you built something different. How did you know, and how did it go?

**Why we ask it.** This is the judgment half. Requests are symptoms. We want to see them
diagnose.

**What a strong answer covers.** A concrete request, the conversation or observation that
revealed the underlying problem, and what they built instead. Critically: how they checked
with the customer before or after. Whether it actually solved the problem, and how they
know.

**Follow-ups.** "How did you find out what they actually needed?" "Did you tell them you
were building something different, or did they find out when it shipped?" "Did they agree
it was better?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They overrode the customer on instinct and never checked. The
"different thing" was what the engineer wanted to build. They cannot say whether it
worked.

### 2. Tell me about the last time you watched a user use your software. What did you see that surprised you?

**Why we ask it.** The single strongest predictor of customer focus is whether they have
done this at all, and recently.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific session — a customer call, a support
shadowing, a usability session, a screen recording. Something specific they saw that they
had not expected, and what they changed because of it. Ideally it is small and
embarrassing: a button nobody could find, a workflow done in a spreadsheet next to the
product.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you change?" "How often do you do this?" "What did you see that
you have not fixed yet?"

**Where this goes wrong.** Never, or years ago. They watched but nothing surprised them.
The observation did not lead to a change.

### 3. Tell me about a time you had to say no to a customer request. How did you say it, and how did they respond?

**Why we ask it.** Customer-centric does not mean customer-driven. A product that says yes
to everything becomes unusable. We want to see them decline well.

**What a strong answer covers.** A request that was reasonable from the customer's side
but wrong for the product — a one-off, a workaround, something that would break for other
customers. They explained the reasoning in the customer's terms, offered what they could
do, and kept the relationship. They can say whether the customer was right in the end.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you offer instead?" "Did they push back?" "Have you ever said no
and been wrong?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The no came from "not on the roadmap" with no reasoning. They
have never said no. They said no to the customer and yes to a louder customer.

### 4. Describe a time you argued for building something the customers had not asked for. What was the evidence, and were you right?

**Why we ask it.** The second half of the principle. We want to see whether they can make
a customer-value case for something without a ticket behind it.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific gap they observed — in usage data, in support
tickets, in watching users — that customers had not articulated. How they made the case.
What they built. Whether customers used it, and how they measured that. An honest answer
where they were wrong is fine.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the evidence?" "Who did you have to convince?" "How did you
know afterward whether it mattered?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The evidence was "I thought it would be cool". They cannot say
whether it was used. Every story ends with the customers loving it.

### 5. Tell me about a time a customer was frustrated with something you built. What did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Controllers under close pressure are direct. We want to see how they
handle an unhappy expert user without getting defensive or folding.

**What a strong answer covers.** They talked to the customer directly, or listened to the
call. They separated the frustration from the underlying issue. They fixed what was
fixable, explained what was not, and followed up. They can describe what the customer said
in the customer's words.

**Follow-ups.** "What exactly did they say?" "Were they right?" "What did you tell them
you would do, and did you do it?"

**Where this goes wrong.** Support handled it. The customer was wrong and that is the end
of the story. They fixed it but never closed the loop.

### 6. How do you learn what a user's job actually looks like when it is nothing like yours?

**Why we ask it.** Most engineers we hire have never done a close. This question asks how
they close that gap, and whether they think it needs closing.

**What a strong answer covers.** Concrete habits — reading support tickets, sitting in on
onboarding, asking the customer-facing team, doing the workflow themselves in a sandbox,
reading what practitioners read. An example of something they learned this way that
changed what they built. Humility about how much they still do not know.

**Follow-ups.** "Give me an example of something you got wrong because you did not
understand the job." "What do you do in your first month on a new domain?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They rely entirely on the product manager. They assume the job
is simpler than it is. They have no example.
