# Growth Mindset

> Embrace challenges as opportunities for long-term success and continuous learning.

## What we are listening for

Almost nobody we hire knows accounting when they arrive. Most of what we build has not
been built before. So we need people who can start from zero in a domain, ask questions
that make them look uninformed, be wrong in public, and come back the next week better.
We also need people who help others do the same.

The specific things we listen for:

- Learning that shows up as changed behavior, not as a reading list.
- Comfort being the least knowledgeable person in the room and saying so.
- Taking on the thing they were not sure they could do, and describing the part that was
  hard.
- Treating feedback as information rather than as a verdict.
- Getting better at something they were bad at, with specifics.

We are not screening for enthusiasm. Someone who says they love learning and has no
example of changing their mind is not showing us a growth mindset.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a time you took on something you did not know how to do. How did you get from not knowing to shipping?

**Why we ask it.** This is what the first six months here look like. We want to see the
method, not the outcome.

**What a strong answer covers.** A real gap — a domain, a language, a system they had
never touched. What they did on the first day. Who they asked, what they read, what they
built to learn. The point where they were most stuck and what got them through. What they
would do faster now.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the first thing you did?" "Who did you ask and what did you ask
them?" "What did you get wrong along the way?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The gap was small. The story is about talent rather than
method. They cannot name anyone who helped.

### 2. Tell me about a piece of feedback that was hard to hear. What was it, and what did you do with it?

**Why we ask it.** Growth requires input. We want to see them take something that stung
and turn it into a change.

**What a strong answer covers.** Specific feedback, ideally about how they work with
others rather than about code. Their honest first reaction. What they concluded after
sitting with it. A specific change in behavior and evidence it stuck — someone noticing,
a later situation handled differently.

**Follow-ups.** "What was your first reaction?" "Did you agree with it?" "What do you do
differently now?" "Would the person who gave it say you changed?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The feedback was about a technical skill and the fix was a
course. They disagreed with it and still do. They cannot think of any.

### 3. Tell me about something you used to believe strongly about building software that you no longer believe. What changed your mind?

**Why we ask it.** Growth is visible as changed opinions. This tells us whether they
update on evidence or on fashion.

**What a strong answer covers.** A real position they held and argued for. The specific
experience or evidence that moved them. Where they landed, which is usually more nuanced
than the opposite position. They can still explain why the original belief was
attractive.

**Follow-ups.** "What convinced you?" "Is there anything you still think the old view got
right?" "What do you believe now that you suspect you will change your mind on?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They changed with the industry consensus and cannot say why.
The belief was trivial. They have never changed their mind about anything important.

### 4. Tell me about a time you were the least experienced person on a team or project. How did you contribute, and how did you handle not knowing things?

**Why we ask it.** Everyone joining us is this person on the accounting side. We want to
know they can be useful and honest at the same time.

**What a strong answer covers.** They asked questions early rather than hiding the gap.
They found a way to contribute that did not require the expertise they lacked — writing
things down, testing, taking the unglamorous piece. They can name a question they were
embarrassed to ask and asked anyway.

**Follow-ups.** "What was a question you were nervous to ask?" "How long before you felt
useful?" "What did the experienced people get from you?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They pretended to know. They waited to contribute until they
had caught up. They describe the experienced people as unhelpful.

### 5. What is something you are actively trying to get better at right now, and what are you doing about it?

**Why we ask it.** Present tense. It distinguishes people who have a practice from people
who have a history.

**What a strong answer covers.** Something specific and somewhat uncomfortable to admit —
giving hard feedback, estimating, writing, reading code they did not write, listening.
Concrete actions in the last month. Some way they will know if it is working.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you do about it this month?" "How will you know it worked?"
"Who knows you are working on it?"

**Where this goes wrong.** A technology on their list to learn. A strength disguised as a
weakness. Nothing.

### 6. Tell me about a time you helped someone else grow. What did they need, and what did you do?

**Why we ask it.** A growth mindset that stops at oneself is only half useful on a team.
We want people who create it around them.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific person — a junior engineer, a peer moving
domains, a new hire. What they noticed the person needed, which is not always what the
person asked for. What they did — pairing, review, giving them the hard task with a
safety net, stepping back. What happened to that person.

**Follow-ups.** "How did you know what they needed?" "Was there a point where you had to
let them fail?" "Where are they now?"

**Where this goes wrong.** Helping meant doing it for them. The story is about the
candidate's generosity rather than the other person's growth. No outcome.
