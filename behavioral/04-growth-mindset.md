# Growth Mindset

> Embrace challenges as opportunities for long-term success and continuous learning.

## What we are listening for

Most people we hire do not know accounting when they arrive. Much of what we build has
not been built before. So we need people who can start from zero in a domain, ask
questions that might make them look uninformed, be wrong in front of others, and come
back the next week better. We also need people who help others do the same.

The specific things we listen for:

- Learning that shows up as changed behavior, not just as a reading list.
- Being comfortable as the least knowledgeable person in the room and saying so.
- Taking on something they were not sure they could do, and describing the part that was
  hard.
- Treating feedback as information rather than as a verdict.
- Getting better at something they were not good at, with specifics.

We are not screening for enthusiasm. Saying you love learning tells us less than one
example of changing your mind.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a time you took on something you did not know how to do. How did you get from not knowing to shipping?

**Why we ask it.** This is what the first six months here look like. We want to hear
about the method more than the outcome.

**What a strong answer covers.** A real gap, such as a domain, a language, or a system
they had never touched. What they did on the first day. Who they asked, what they read,
what they built to learn. The point where they were most stuck and what got them through.
What they would do faster now.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the first thing you did?" "Who did you ask and what did you ask
them?" "What did you get wrong along the way?"

**Weaker answers.** The gap was small. The story is about talent rather than method. They
cannot name anyone who helped.

### 2. Tell me about a piece of feedback that was hard to hear. What was it, and what did you do with it?

**Why we ask it.** Growth requires input from others. We want to see them take something
that was hard to hear and turn it into a change.

**What a strong answer covers.** Specific feedback, ideally about how they work with
others rather than about code. Their honest first reaction. What they concluded after
thinking it over. A specific change in behavior and evidence it lasted, such as someone
noticing or a later situation handled differently.

**Follow-ups.** "What was your first reaction?" "Did you agree with it?" "What do you do
differently now?" "Would the person who gave it say you changed?"

**Weaker answers.** The feedback was about a technical skill and the fix was a course.
They disagreed with it and still do. They cannot think of any.

### 3. Tell me about something you used to believe strongly about building software that you no longer believe. What changed your mind?

**Why we ask it.** Changed opinions are one way growth shows. This tells us whether they
update based on evidence or based on what is popular.

**What a strong answer covers.** A real position they held and argued for. The specific
experience or evidence that moved them. Where they landed, which is usually more nuanced
than the opposite position. They can still explain why the original belief was
appealing.

**Follow-ups.** "What convinced you?" "Is there anything you still think the old view got
right?" "What do you believe now that you suspect you will change your mind on?"

**Weaker answers.** They changed with the industry consensus and cannot say why. The
belief was minor. They have never changed their mind about anything important.

### 4. Tell me about a time you were the least experienced person on a team or project. How did you contribute, and how did you handle not knowing things?

**Why we ask it.** Everyone who joins us is in this position on the accounting side. We
want to know they can be useful and honest at the same time.

**What a strong answer covers.** They asked questions early rather than hiding the gap.
They found a way to contribute that did not need the expertise they lacked, such as
writing things down, testing, or taking the less visible piece. They can name a question
they were nervous to ask and asked anyway.

**Follow-ups.** "What was a question you were nervous to ask?" "How long before you felt
useful?" "What did the experienced people get from you?"

**Weaker answers.** They pretended to know. They waited to contribute until they had
caught up. They describe the experienced people as unhelpful.

### 5. What is something you are actively trying to get better at right now, and what are you doing about it?

**Why we ask it.** This question is about the present. It tells us whether they have an
ongoing practice or only a history.

**What a strong answer covers.** Something specific and a little uncomfortable to admit,
such as giving hard feedback, estimating, writing, reading code they did not write, or
listening. Concrete actions in the last month. Some way they will know if it is working.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you do about it this month?" "How will you know it worked?"
"Who knows you are working on it?" "Is there something AI tools have made you better at,
or worse at?"

**Weaker answers.** A technology on their list to learn. A strength presented as a
weakness. They have nothing in progress.

### 6. Tell me about a time you helped someone else grow. What did they need, and what did you do?

**Why we ask it.** A growth mindset that stops with oneself is only half as useful on a
team. We want people who help create it around them.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific person, such as a junior engineer, a peer
moving domains, or a new hire. What they noticed the person needed, which is not always
what the person asked for. What they did: pairing, review, giving them the hard task with
a safety net, stepping back. What happened to that person.

**Follow-ups.** "How did you know what they needed?" "Was there a point where you had to
let them fail?" "Where are they now?"

**Weaker answers.** Helping meant doing it for them. The story is about the candidate's
generosity rather than the other person's growth. There is no outcome.

### 7. Tell me about a skill you have had to relearn or rethink because of AI tools.

**Why we ask it.** The job is changing, and people who keep up are rethinking how they
work rather than just adding a tool. We want to see that reflection.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific skill: writing code from scratch, reading
code they did not write, specifying a task precisely, reviewing, debugging output they
did not produce. How their approach has changed. Something they have chosen to keep
practicing by hand, and why. Something they have let go of.

**Follow-ups.** "What do you still do by hand on purpose?" "What have you stopped doing?"
"How do you keep your own judgment sharp when the tool does the first draft?"

**Weaker answers.** Nothing has changed for them. They have stopped practicing everything
the tool can do. The answer is about tools rather than about their own skills.
