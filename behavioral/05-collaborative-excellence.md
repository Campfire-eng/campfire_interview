# Collaborative Excellence

> Foster teamwork and shared accountability to achieve outstanding results.

## What we are listening for

An ERP is one product with many hands in it. The ledger, the bank feed, the close, the
reporting, and Ember all have to agree with each other, and no one person holds all of
it. We want engineers who make the people around them better, who treat a teammate's
failure as a shared one, and who can disagree strongly about the work without it becoming
about the people.

"Shared accountability" is a phrase to take seriously. It means that if the team's release
broke, it is not enough to say that your part worked.

The specific things we listen for:

- Disagreements that stayed about the problem and ended with a decision.
- Picking up work that was not theirs because it was blocking the team.
- Giving credit to the specific people who earned it, and sharing the blame when things
  go wrong.
- Communicating across a boundary, such as another team, a non-engineer, or a remote
  colleague, and adapting to how the other side works.
- Helping a team decision stick after it was made, including when they lost.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a serious technical disagreement with a teammate. How did it get resolved, and how was the relationship afterward?

**Why we ask it.** This is the most common way collaboration breaks down. We want to see
them argue well and stop arguing when it is time.

**What a strong answer covers.** A disagreement that mattered, such as architecture, a
data model, or whether to rewrite. They can state the other person's position fairly, not
as a strawman. How it got decided: evidence, a prototype, an agreed decider. What they did
after losing, if they lost. The relationship survived, and they can say how.

**Follow-ups.** "Make their argument for me." "Who decided, and how?" "Were you right?"
"How did you work together the month after?"

**Weaker answers.** The other person was simply wrong. It was resolved by escalation with
no attempt to resolve it directly. It was never resolved. They no longer work with that
person and the story explains why.

### 2. Tell me about a time the team failed at something and your part had worked. What did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Shared accountability is easy to say. This is the situation that shows
whether it is practiced.

**What a strong answer covers.** They treated the failure as theirs too. They helped fix
the part that broke, even if it was not their code. In the retrospective, they looked for
what they could have done, such as asking about integration earlier, reviewing the other
piece, or flagging the risk. They do not make a point of their part having worked.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say in the retro?" "What could you have done earlier?" "How
did the person whose part broke feel about how the team handled it?"

**Weaker answers.** The story is about how their part worked. They fixed nothing outside
their area. They cannot name anything they would have done differently.

### 3. Tell me about working with someone whose style was very different from yours. What was hard, and what did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Teams are made of different kinds of people. We want people who adapt
without resentment.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific difference, such as pace, communication,
planning versus improvising, or written versus verbal. Something they changed about how
they worked with that person. Something they learned from the difference. Respect for the
other person's approach, not just tolerance of it.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you change?" "What did they do well that you do not?" "Did you
talk to them about it directly?"

**Weaker answers.** The other person needed to change. They worked around the person
rather than with them. They describe the difference as a shortcoming.

### 4. Tell me about a time you had to get something done that depended on another team, and that team had other priorities.

**Why we ask it.** Cross-team work is where many projects stall. We want people who can
move it forward without pulling rank or going around people.

**What a strong answer covers.** They understood the other team's priorities and why
theirs was not at the top. They made the case in the other team's terms, found a smaller
ask, or did some of the work themselves. They kept the relationship. They did not escalate
as the first step.

**Follow-ups.** "What was their reason for the priority?" "What did you offer them?" "Did
you escalate, and at what point?"

**Weaker answers.** Escalation was the first step. The other team is described as an
obstacle. They built a workaround that the other team then had to maintain.

### 5. Tell me about a time you gave a teammate feedback that was difficult to give. How did you do it, and what happened?

**Why we ask it.** Teams that do not give each other feedback do not improve. We want
people who will do the uncomfortable thing directly and kindly.

**What a strong answer covers.** Something that mattered, not a nitpick. They gave it
directly to the person, privately, soon after, with specifics and without judgment about
character. They can say roughly what they said. They can say how the person responded
and whether things changed.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say, roughly?" "How long did you wait before saying it?"
"How did they respond?" "Would you do it the same way again?"

**Weaker answers.** They told the manager instead. They waited until a review cycle. It
was delivered in front of others. They have never done it.

### 6. Tell me about a time you helped a team you were on become better at working together, not just at shipping.

**Why we ask it.** Collaborative excellence is a property of the team, and someone has to
build it. We want to see whether they take that on.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific problem with how the team worked, such as
reviews sitting for days, decisions being reopened, standups that were only status
readouts, or someone being left out. Something they did about it, small and concrete.
Evidence it changed. Ideally they did it without a title that required them to.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the problem, specifically?" "What did you try first?" "What
happened?" "Did it stick after you stopped pushing?"

**Weaker answers.** The change was a process imposed from above. They noticed the problem
and waited for someone else. The improvement is claimed with no evidence.

### 7. Tell me about a time you got credit for something a teammate deserved, or a teammate got credit for something you did.

**Why we ask it.** How people handle credit shows whether shared accountability is
something they practice.

**What a strong answer covers.** For misdirected credit: they corrected it, publicly if it
was given publicly. For uncredited work: they can describe it without bitterness, and
either let it go for a good reason or raised it in a reasonable way. Either way, the story
is proportionate.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say?" "How did the other person react?" "Does it still
bother you?"

**Weaker answers.** They kept the credit. The uncredited story is told with lingering
anger. They have never paid attention to who gets credit.
