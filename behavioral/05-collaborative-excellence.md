# Collaborative Excellence

> Foster teamwork and shared accountability to achieve outstanding results.

## What we are listening for

An ERP is one product with many hands in it. The ledger, the bank feed, the close, the
reporting, and Ember all have to agree with each other, and no one person holds all of
it. We want engineers who make the people around them better, who treat a teammate's
failure as a shared one, and who can disagree hard about the work without it becoming
about the people.

"Shared accountability" is the phrase to take seriously. It means if the team's release
broke, you do not get to be the person whose part worked.

The specific things we listen for:

- Disagreements that stayed about the problem and ended with a decision.
- Picking up work that was not theirs because it was blocking the team.
- Giving credit precisely and taking blame broadly.
- Communicating across a boundary — another team, a non-engineer, a remote colleague —
  and adapting to how the other side works.
- Making a team decision stick after it was made, including when they lost.

## Questions

### 1. Tell me about a serious technical disagreement with a teammate. How did it get resolved, and how was the relationship afterward?

**Why we ask it.** This is the most common way collaboration fails. We want to see them
argue well and stop arguing when it is time.

**What a strong answer covers.** A disagreement that mattered — architecture, a data
model, whether to rewrite. They can state the other person's position fairly, better than
a strawman. How it got decided: evidence, a prototype, an agreed decider. What they did
after losing, if they lost. The relationship survived, and they can say how.

**Follow-ups.** "Make their argument for me." "Who decided, and how?" "Were you right?"
"How did you work together the month after?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The other person was simply wrong. It was resolved by
escalation with no attempt to resolve it directly. It was never resolved. They do not work
with that person anymore and the story is why.

### 2. Tell me about a time the team failed at something and your part had worked. What did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Shared accountability is easy to say. This is the situation that tests
it.

**What a strong answer covers.** They treated the failure as theirs. They helped fix the
part that broke, even if it was not their code. In the retrospective, they looked for what
they could have done — asked about integration earlier, reviewed the other piece, flagged
the risk. They do not make a point of their part having worked.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say in the retro?" "What could you have done earlier?" "How
did the person whose part broke feel about how the team handled it?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The story is about how their part worked. They fixed nothing
outside their area. They cannot name anything they would have done differently.

### 3. Tell me about working with someone whose style was very different from yours. What was hard, and what did you do?

**Why we ask it.** Teams are not made of clones. We want people who adapt without
resentment.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific difference — pace, communication, planning
versus improvising, written versus verbal. Something they changed about how they worked
with that person. Something they learned from the difference. Respect for the other
person's approach, not just tolerance.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you change?" "What did they do well that you do not?" "Did you
talk to them about it directly?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The other person needed to change. They worked around the
person rather than with them. They describe the difference as a deficiency.

### 4. Tell me about a time you had to get something done that depended on another team, and that team had other priorities.

**Why we ask it.** Cross-team work is where most projects stall. We want people who can
move it without pulling rank or going around people.

**What a strong answer covers.** They understood the other team's priorities and why
theirs was not at the top. They made the case in the other team's terms, found a smaller
ask, or did some of the work themselves. They kept the relationship. They did not escalate
as the first move.

**Follow-ups.** "What was their reason for the priority?" "What did you offer them?" "Did
you escalate, and at what point?"

**Where this goes wrong.** Escalation was the first move. The other team is described as
an obstacle. They built a workaround that the other team then had to maintain.

### 5. Tell me about a time you gave a teammate feedback that was difficult to give. How did you do it, and what happened?

**Why we ask it.** Teams that do not give each other feedback do not get better. We want
people who will do the uncomfortable thing directly and kindly.

**What a strong answer covers.** Something that mattered — not a nitpick. They gave it
directly to the person, privately, soon after, with specifics and without judgment about
character. They can say roughly what they said. They can say how the person responded
and whether things changed.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say, roughly?" "How long did you wait before saying it?"
"How did they respond?" "Would you do it the same way again?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They told the manager instead. They waited until a review
cycle. It was delivered in front of others. They have never done it.

### 6. Tell me about a time you helped a team you were on become better at working together, not just at shipping.

**Why we ask it.** Collaborative excellence is a property of the team, and someone has to
build it. We want to see whether they take that on.

**What a strong answer covers.** A specific problem with how the team worked — reviews
sat for days, decisions got relitigated, standups were status theatre, someone was being
left out. Something they did about it, small and concrete. Evidence it changed. Ideally
they did it without a title that required them to.

**Follow-ups.** "What was the problem, specifically?" "What did you try first?" "What
happened?" "Did it stick after you stopped pushing?"

**Where this goes wrong.** The change was a process imposed from above. They noticed the
problem and waited for someone else. The improvement is asserted with no evidence.

### 7. Tell me about a time you got credit for something a teammate deserved, or a teammate got credit for something you did.

**Why we ask it.** How people handle credit reveals whether "shared accountability" is a
value or a slogan.

**What a strong answer covers.** For misdirected credit: they corrected it, publicly if it
was given publicly. For uncredited work: they can describe it without bitterness, and
either let it go for a good reason or raised it well. Either way, the story is
proportionate.

**Follow-ups.** "What did you say?" "How did the other person react?" "Does it still
bother you?"

**Where this goes wrong.** They kept the credit. The uncredited story is told with heat.
They have never noticed credit flowing at all.
